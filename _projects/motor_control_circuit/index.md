# Circuit Design
At a higher level, the aim of the circuit is to convert the angular speed of a DC motor into a proportional analog voltage, against which a feedback loop corrects about an adjustable pre-set voltage, regulating the current flow through the DC motor.

But how can we translate the speed of a motor into an easily tunable parameter? One might be tempted to look at a datasheet for the motor, where a relationship between speed and current might reveal an easy path to deriving speed from a set-point voltage. However, we need to control speed independent of the torque of the motor, or manufacturing variance across different motors. 

As in many control problems, it's best to measure as close as possible to the source of the controlled variable. So, why not measure the speed of the motor by measuring the angular velocity of its shaft? We achieve this by attaching a black disc to the shaft with equidistant holes at a fixed radius, so that when a hole passes by an externally mounted phototransistor as the shaft spins, it conducts current, generating a clock pulse. From here, the idea is to make the control variable the number of clock pulses generated by the phototransistor module in a fixed interval.

Figure 1 gives the full signal path of the circuit. The phototransistor produces clock pulses from the rotating motor, the counter counts those pulses during a fixed window, the D flip-flop stores the result, the DAC converts that stored digital number into a voltage, and the final analog section compares that measured speed voltage against a setpoint to control the motor.

![Circuit Schematic]({{ page.url | remove: 'index/' | append: 'general_overview.png' | relative_url }})

With this in mind, the design naturally splits into three sub-ideas:

1. How do we count the number of motor pulses in a fixed interval, and how do we govern the timing of that interval?
2. How do we convert this digital count into an analog voltage?
3. How do we use that voltage to close the feedback loop and drive the motor?

## From motor speed to a digital count

For the first problem, we'll use custom digital logic IC's ([74HCT393](https://www.ti.com/lit/ds/symlink/cd74hc393.pdf?ts=1782361521935&ref_url=https%253A%252F%252Fwww.google.com%252F)) to count clock cycles and latch the values with a D octal flip-flop ([74HCT273](https://www.ti.com/lit/ds/symlink/cd74hc273.pdf)) once a timing interval ends. To control the interval timing, we cheat by using a waveform generated 5 Hz square wave. While we could have designed such a generator using a 555 timer, this course emphasized learning to use oscilloscopes, waveform generators, logic and frequency spectrum analyzers and other tools as well as practical circuit design fundamentals.

Every time a hole in the motor disc passes the phototransistor, the counter receives a pulse. Since the latch updates at 5 Hz, each latched value tells us how many pulses occurred in the last 0.2 seconds. With 10 equally spaced holes on the disc, 10 counts corresponds to one full rotation, so

$$
\text{RPM} = \frac{\text{counts}}{10} \cdot \frac{1}{0.2\ \text{s}} \cdot 60 = 30 \cdot \text{counts}.
$$

This is the first useful abstraction in the circuit: motor speed has become a digital number.

In practice, one of the nicest parts of this project was that the digital section was very transparent to debug. We monitored the output of the latch with configured logic analyzers to read decimal values out of the array of high or low signals. This made it much easier to see whether the circuit was counting, resetting, and latching in the order I expected. Using IC's also encouraged us to learn the ins and outs of reading and using datasheets to inform design decisions and aid in debugging. We also learned the digital logic underlying flip-flops and designed and prototyped a simple example by breadboard.

![Logic Analyzer and DAC output]({{ page.url | remove: 'index/' | append: 'logic_analyzer.png' | relative_url }})

One subtlety that needs to be considered is timing. When an interval ends, we need to latch the current counter value to the output of the D flip-flop. But the counter also needs to be reset to zero, so if both things happen at the same time by design, we might end up unintentionally latching zero to the output of the flip-flop! To ensure this doesn't happen, we need a delay.

The delay is introduced by the RC network between the first inverter stage and U1B. The input square wave is first cleaned up by the inverter chain, then passed into an RC charging path before reaching the next logic threshold. Since the capacitor voltage cannot change instantaneously, the input to U1B does not switch at exactly the same time as the original square wave. Instead, it crosses the inverter threshold only after the capacitor has charged or discharged far enough. Symbolically, this delay is set by the RC time constant,

$$
\tau = RC
$$

and by the switching threshold of the inverter. For a rising capacitor voltage, the delay is found from the charging relation,

$$
V_C(t) = V_{DD}(1 - e^{-t/RC})
$$

while for a falling capacitor voltage, it is found from the discharge relation,

$$
V_C(t) = V_0 e^{-t/RC}.
$$

This is the main timing mechanism in the circuit: the logic transition is intentionally shifted by the finite charging time of the capacitor.

The reset pulse is generated in a similar way, but with the RC network configured to produce a short transient rather than a sustained delay. When the delayed logic signal changes state, the capacitor momentarily drives the input of the following inverter across its threshold. As the capacitor then charges or discharges back toward its steady-state value, the input quickly returns below the threshold, ending the reset pulse. In other words, the reset stage converts an edge of the delayed square wave into a narrow pulse whose width is again determined mainly by an RC time constant and the inverter threshold.

Together, these blocks let the circuit count rotations only during well-defined timing windows. The delayed signal controls when the latch captures the current counter value, while the reset pulse clears the counter for the next interval.

## From a digital count to an analog voltage

For the second problem, the digitally stored latch output represents a binary number whose decimal is the number of counts occurring in the past interval. This is where a DAC (Digital to Analog Converter) is needed. Several methods exist, but we used a resistive ladder network; an ingeniously designed recursive DAC. Using techniques learned in ELEC 204, another course from this term, we were able to derive the behaviour of the circuit, in which each outputted bit contributes double the voltage of the last one.

The R-2R ladder is a clever way of doing this with only two resistor values. The reason it works is recursive: looking into the ladder from each node, the rest of the ladder has the same equivalent resistance structure. This causes each successive bit to be divided by another factor of two before reaching the output. For an 8-bit latch output,

$$
N = 128b_7 + 64b_6 + 32b_5 + \cdots + 2b_1 + b_0,
$$

where each $b_i$ is either 0 or 1. Ideally, the DAC output is then proportional to this binary number:

$$
V_\text{DAC} = V_\text{ref} \left(\frac{b_7}{2}+\frac{b_6}{4}+\frac{b_5}{8}+\cdots+\frac{b_0}{256}\right)=V_\text{ref}\frac{N}{256}.
$$

So the digital count is now an analog voltage. The voltage resolution is one count, or

$$
\Delta V = \frac{V_\text{ref}}{256}.
$$

I then buffer this DAC output with an op-amp voltage follower. This is important because the resistor ladder is not meant to drive the rest of the control circuit directly. Without the buffer, the next stage would load the DAC and distort the voltage. The buffer preserves the voltage while providing a much lower output impedance to the error amplifier.

At this point, the circuit has done the main measurement task: it has converted motor speed into a stable analog voltage proportional to RPM. The remaining question is what to do with that voltage.

## Closing the loop

Now we have two voltages: the measured speed voltage from the DAC, and a desired speed voltage set by a potentiometer. The potentiometer acts as an adjustable voltage divider, creating a setpoint voltage $V_\text{set}$. If the motor is too slow, then $V_\text{meas} < V_\text{set}$, and the circuit should increase motor drive. If the motor is too fast, then $V_\text{meas} > V_\text{set}$, and the circuit should reduce motor drive.

This is what the error amplifier does. The op-amp is configured as a proportional-integral controller around the setpoint voltage. Assuming ideal op-amp behaviour, the inverting input is held approximately equal to the non-inverting input, so

$$
V_- \approx V_+ = V_\text{set}.
$$

The current through the input resistor is therefore

$$
i(t) = \frac{V_\text{meas}(t)-V_\text{set}}{R_6}.
$$

Since essentially no current enters the op-amp input, this same current has to pass through the feedback network. The resistor in the feedback path gives an immediate proportional response, while the capacitor gives an accumulated integral response. In time-domain form, the output is approximately

$$
V_\text{ctrl}(t) = V_\text{set} - \frac{R_7}{R_6} \left(V_\text{meas}(t)-V_\text{set}\right) - \frac{1}{R_6C_1} \int \left(V_\text{meas}(t)-V_\text{set}\right)dt.
$$

The signs are worth thinking through. If the motor is too slow, then $V_\text{meas}-V_\text{set}$ is negative. The error amplifier subtracts this negative value, so the control voltage rises. This drives the motor harder. If the motor is too fast, the opposite happens: the measured voltage is larger than the setpoint, and the control voltage decreases.

The proportional term reacts immediately to the current error. The integral term reacts to persistent error. This matters because a DC motor does not always run at the same speed for the same drive voltage: friction, loading, supply limits, and mechanical drag all change the relationship between voltage and RPM. The integral term keeps accumulating correction until the steady-state error is driven toward zero.

![Error Amplification Sub-circuit Hand-drawn Diagram]({{ page.url | remove: 'index/' | append: 'error_amp_circ_diag.jpeg' | relative_url }})

The final stage is the motor drive stage. The op-amp should not directly power the motor: the motor is an inductive, relatively high-current load, while the op-amp is a signal-level device. Instead, the op-amp output controls the base of a power transistor, and the transistor controls the much larger current through the motor.

This is the small but important power-electronics part of the circuit. The motor current flows through the motor and the power transistor, rather than through the op-amp. The transistor acts like a current valve: a small control signal at its input changes the much larger current available to the motor. The flyback diode across the motor is also important, because the motor winding is inductive. When the transistor suddenly reduces current, the motor winding resists that change and can generate a large voltage spike. The diode gives that current a safe path, protecting the transistor and the rest of the circuit.

There is also a low-value resistor in the motor current path. This resistor is not there to waste power; it gives the circuit some local current feedback and makes the power stage less abrupt. As motor current increases, the voltage across this resistor increases, which reduces the effective drive across the transistor input. This helps prevent the control loop from becoming a purely open-loop “slam the motor harder” stage.

With the corrected error amplifier values, the behaviour became what the control-loop picture predicts: turning the potentiometer changes the desired speed, loading the motor causes the controller to increase drive, and once the load is removed the feedback loop settles the motor back toward the setpoint.

# Assembly, Debugging, Lessons learned

Assembly was done by section, and each circuit was tested and trouble-shooted in isolation before connecting sub-circuits together. Systematic assembly and testing in this way is extremely important; building in layers of complexity lets you rule out more rudimentary issues, and put more confidence in troubleshooting integration-related issues. As well, intimate troubleshooting and debugging forces you to understand the sub-circuits very well, which is critical for each layer of complexity added.

Planar cut-to-length wiring, minimizing total wire length, and mimicking the logical sequence of the circuit in the breadboard layout were principles I applied to ease troubleshooting and make it easier for others to follow along the circuit from the breadboard. For each feature I added on the breadboard, I hand-drew a circuit sub-diagram, including references to datasheet pinouts, and I took a photo and labelled that photo with reference to the chip datasheets and the circuit schematic, so that I could systematically confirm wiring.

![Error Amplification Labelled Breadboard Photo]({{ page.url | remove: 'index/' | append: 'error_amp_labelled.jpeg' | relative_url }})

The most useful debugging came from treating the circuit as a chain of testable transformations. The phototransistor should generate pulses. The counter should turn pulses into a binary count. The latch should freeze that count at the end of the timing window. The DAC should turn the frozen count into a voltage. The buffer should preserve that voltage. The error amplifier should move in the correct direction relative to the setpoint. Finally, the transistor stage should translate the control signal into motor current.

That way of thinking made faults much easier to isolate. For example, when I accidentally powered an op-amp incorrectly, the buffer output was obviously wrong compared to the DAC output I had already verified. Later, when the motor control was far too abrupt, the issue ended up being a wrong resistor value in the error amplifier. At signal level, a wrong resistor value can make a waveform look strange. At motor-power level, a wrong assumption can make components heat up very quickly. That forced me to stop treating the motor as just another schematic block and start checking current paths, voltage drops, and power dissipation before trusting the circuit.

# Lessons Learned

- Documentation is just as critical as critical thinking in problem solving, and long-term project success.
- Double check power calculations when running power electronics.
- Spending an extra 30 minutes setting up a systematic debugging protocol / setup almost always outperforms guess and check troubleshooting.
- You understand something well once you can explain it to someone else from the bottom up.
